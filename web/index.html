HOW IT WORKS

Three parts, none of them an animation

01 // THE GLASSES SENSE

The glasses scan your room, ask an AI what is in it, and turn that into what a fly has organs for: a smell with a direction, a shape that looms, a surface to land on, a moving hand. Point at a thing and it starts to smell. The fly’s two compound eyes are real cameras, sampled facet by facet — 1,767 of them, like the real eye.

Two of those senses are nudged on purpose, and written down: the loom is turned down within 70 cm of something the fly could land on, or the table under the apple would scare it off, and food smells up to 2.5× stronger while it is hungry. Nothing nudges what it does about them.

02 // THE BRAIN DECIDES

The senses enter the brain exactly where they enter a real fly’s, and the whole brain is run forward, 50 ms at a time. Out comes the firing of the 1,332 neurons that carry orders from the brain to the body. There is no script to read; the spikes are the decision.

03 // THE BODY OBEYS

Wings, legs, head and tongue follow the same nerve groups as in the animal. The fly turns because the steering neuron on one side fired; it eats because the feeding neuron did. When it refuses, that is the brain refusing.

Small print: the steering cell is DNa02, the feeding cell MN9 — the atlas’s own names, shown on the dashboard.

WHY A PAGE

The glasses are too small to think this fast: one 50 ms slice of fly life cost them a second or two. A laptop’s graphics card does it in about 100 ms. So type the PIN and this page becomes the brain; the glasses stay the body.

WHAT YOU SEE

All 166,700 neurons, each lit the moment it fires, and signals running along the real connections. Beside it: what the eyes see, the room, and the fly’s decision in words.

140,638 neurons are drawn where the atlas measured them; the rest are placed by their partners and drawn dimmer, so the picture never pretends.

LEARNING

Nobody trains it. It learns from what happens to it.

WHAT IT LEARNS // A SMELL

It does not learn “a mug”. A thing’s name becomes one of a handful of smells the fly can tell apart (four in the released brain file, sixteen in the newer export), and that smell is all it ever has of the thing. What moves the memory is dopamine, the fly’s own reward and punishment: 15 cells for reward, 2 for punishment. While they fire, the connections between that smell and go / don’t-go shift.

Two things can land on the same smell, and then the fly genuinely cannot tell them apart. The rings around a thing on the dashboard are coloured by its smell, so you can watch that happen. Small print: the smells are the glomeruli VA2, DM4, DM2, DM1; the cells PAM11 and PPL101.

IT LEARNS ON ITS OWN // ALL THE TIME

There is no lesson to run and no button to press. The fly lives in your room and its memory centre changes from what happens to it. Measured over ten minutes with nobody teaching it: 4,119 of its 7,835 memory connections had moved, and their average strength had drifted from 1.0000 to 0.9599.

Point at a thing, or touch it, and it starts to smell. That is the whole gesture.

HOW YOU CAN TELL // THE MEMORY PANEL

The dashboard’s MEMORY panel counts it live: how many of the 7,835 memory connections have moved and by how much, and when that memory was last saved. The fly keeps it between sessions, so the fly you meet tomorrow is the one that lived in your room today.

A memory that has drifted is not the same as a lesson learned. The page shows the numbers and never claims more than they say.
